                 Finding the Fifth Dimension:
                  a Guide to 5D Chess Strategy

Hello!
This guide is meant to introduce the strategy of competitive 5D Chess with Multiverse Time Travel. It starts from the basic
ideas of how to use time travel to your advantage, and goes all the way to analysis of complicated many-timeline positions.
You can use the guide to learn what concepts to focus on, but there is no substitute for playing games and trying out these
strategies yourself.
Before starting this guide, you'll need to understand the basic rules of the game. Learn how the pieces move, both on the
board and through time. Learn the rules about how new timelines are created. If you see a piece giving check to a king in
the past, do you understand why that has happened? Once you are comfortable with those concepts, you should be ready
for this guide. If you don't understand those things yet, you can watch some explanatory videos [1] or ask people in the 5D
Chess discord server.
Let's dive into the multiverse!

                         FIFTH
The most important skill in 5D chess

This game involves time travel! This fact, unsurprisingly, makes it totally different from normal chess. So we should think
carefully about how to use time travel to our advantage.
There are two facts to consider:

    1. Time travel is very strong! There are many situations where, if you can travel a piece close to your opponent's king,
         then you will be able to force a checkmate.

                                                                                A forced mating sequence after a travel

    2. The rules of 5D chess limit how many timelines you can make. In 5D chess, you are only allowed to make up to one
         more timeline than your opponent has made; any more timelines will be inactive.

The conflict between these two facts brings us to the most important concept of 5D chess strategy: Timeline Advantage. A
time travel can be a good move, but every time you make a new timeline, you are giving your opponent the opportunity to
create a new timeline. If a player has made less timelines than the opponent, we say that player has "Timeline Advantage".
For an analogy: imagine if, in a soccer game, every time you tried to make a shot on the opponent's goal, you gave them a
free penalty kick. Then, you should be very careful about when you try to score, to make sure you get a point!
It's the same in 5D chess: you should only make a travel if you are very confident that it will win you the game, or at least
give a very strong advantage. There is some debate about the exact value of a timeline, but most players agree that timeline
advantage is worth more than a queen.

                                                 Black made a useless travel to capture a bishop. Now white has timeline advantage

Most 5D chess games between strong players are won or lost because one player made a time travel that they thought was
good, but it actually wasn't strong enough to win. So they gave timeline advantage to the opponent, and a few moves later
the opponent makes an even stronger travel to win the game.
It can happen the other way around too: you see that your opponent has a time travel, but it doesn't look very good, so you
don't bother to defend it. But then they make the travel, and it turns out there was a move you didn't see, and now you lose.
The most important skill in 5D chess is the ability to see if a travel is good or bad.
How to make a strong travel

So, now that we know that we should only make strong travels... what does a strong travel look like?
Kings are a lot easier to checkmate in 5D chess. This is because all you need to do to mate is give a single check on a past
king (without your piece being taken). When a past king is in check, and there is nothing that can be done about it on that
timeline, we call it softmate. The only way to escape softmate is to travel back in time from another timeline and hope to
eventually take the piece that is causing the softmate.
Thus, king safety is even more important in 5D chess than normal chess.
The best travels are ones that expose the opponent's king. If a king is brought into the open, it is a lot easier to get a past
check on it.

                                                Black's travel forces the king to step up to e2, where it can be softmated by the knight

In the example above, black sacrifices a queen to bring white's king closer to the center of the board, resulting in a
softmate.
Often, a good travel involves sacrificing a piece to force the opponents king to step into the open, then your other pieces can
give "stall checks" to wait until enough turns have passed that you can give a past check.
                              White travels to f7 to expose the king, then gives a series of checks to stall until the queen can softmate from b3

But remember, the travel needs to work. You can't just throw pieces at the king and hope for a softmate. If the travel
doesn't end with you winning the game, then all you did was give timeline advantage to your opponent. There is no such
thing as a travel that "almost works". If you can't think of how to win after the travel, you probably shouldn't go for it.

Seeing the difference between a winning travel and a useless travel is critical, and is best practiced by playing lots of games
and analyzing lots of positions.
Weaknesses

We can't just look at every possible travel move to see if it will win; there are too many to count. But we know that even a
small weakness can lead to a game-winning travel. So we should look for weak points in the opponent's position, and
then figure out if we can attack them.
Here is a short list of weaknesses to look for:

    1. An open line to the king
If there is an open path to the king, then we can attack it with a queen or bishop. From the starting position, there are 3
ways to get to the king. Check to see if any of those are open.

                                                                        The three paths to the king in the starting position
    2. An undefended pawn next to the king
If there is an undefended pawn next to the king (usually the f-pawn), then we might be able to travel onto that pawn to
start a softmating sequence. The king alone is not enough to defend, because if the pawn is taken the king will be exposed
after recapturing.

                                                                          White has an undefended pawn next to the king

    3. A knight close to the enemy king
If we have a knight close to the enemy king, then there are many ways to attack. The goal is to get the knight within one or
two spaces of the king without it being taken.
There are many more weaknesses, but they are more subtle and require advanced tactics to exploit. I will talk about some
of them later, but you can look at Mage's tactics guide [2] for more information on these.
Opening Theory

Note: this guide focuses on the variant "Standard ­ Turn Zero", because that is the most commonly played competitive variant,
for reasons discussed later in this section.
Now we can begin to think about what the best opening moves are. Look at the starting position of the board:

                                                                 Standard - Turn Zero, the most common competitive variant

Already, there is a weakness in the starting position. Can you see it?
The weakness in the starting position is that there is an undefended pawn next to the king (Weakness #2).

                                                                              Undefended pawns in the starting position

This weakness can be exploited. In fact, if either player can travel a queen to take that pawn on the first turn, then there
will be a forced checkmate!

                                                                White has forced softmate after sacrificing a queen onto T1 f7

The above image shows how white can checkmate after traveling a queen onto f7 on T1. There are many possibilities for
how black can try to escape this, but they all lead to mate unless black specifically prepared for this by setting up pieces on
the original timeline. Here are a few examples of black trying and failing to survive:
                       Some failed attempts at surviving the f7 sacrifice. There is no way for black to survive without help from another timeline

You can read more about this in the Steam guide on the f7 sacrifice [3]. Or, even better, try playing the moves yourself and
figuring out how both sides can try to attack & defend.

The point is, it's very bad to leave the f-pawn undefended. Because the f7 sacrifice is so powerful, almost all of opening
theory is about defending against it, or attacking your opponent with it, or finding other tricky ways to survive it. This is the
reason why the "Standard" variant is considered to be unbalanced, because only white can make this powerful travel to the
first turn of the game. There are several variants which try to fix this imbalance:

     Standard ­ Turn Zero has an additional black board before the first white board, so black can also travel to the first
         turn.

     Standard ­ Defended Pawn rearranges the starting position so that all the pawns near the king are defended
         (removing the weakness).

     Standard ­ Princess replaces the queen with a Princess, which limits its attacking power.

Of these three variants, Turn Zero is the most commonly played in a competitive setting, and thus is the focus of this guide.
However, I encourage you to try other variants to broaden your understanding of the game.
The most common opening strategy in Turn Zero is to defend the f-pawn with the following moves:

                                      The Classical f7 Defense. Both players can play this, because both players need to defend the f-pawn

The first move with the knight blocks one path to f2. Then the d4 pawn move blocks the second path to f7. Finally, the c3
pawn move blocks the path to the king. It's okay to open a path to the king, as long as you remember to defend it soon after.
This opening, the "Classical f7 Defense", is the most common opening, played by beginners and world champions alike. If
you only want to play one opening, you should probably pick this one. But there are many other viable possibilities, and I
encourage you to look into openings to find which ones appeal to your style of play.
Investigating opening moves by playing them on a board against yourself is a great way to get better at the game and
practice your position-analyzing skills. Just remember, whatever opening you play should either defend against the f7
sacrifice, or have a plan for how to counterattack if you get f7-sac'd.

More information on this opening and many others can be found in the T0 Openings Guide [4].
Playing on One Timeline

Because of the concept of timeline advantage, many games take place on just one timeline for most of the game. But don't
be fooled! This is still a very different game from normal 2D chess.

Here are a few miscellaneous notes on how to play well on one timeline:

Every move should have a purpose:
     Defend a weakness in your position
              o If there is a path to your king, block it
              o If there is an undefended pawn, defend it
              o If there is an enemy piece near your king, take it with another piece
     Attack a weakness in your opponent's position
              o Threaten to travel onto a weak pawn
              o If there are no weaknesses in your opponents position, try to create one
     Develop a piece to a better position

If you make a move without a purpose, it's as if you are giving your opponent a free extra turn. In this game, one turn is
often the difference between winning and losing. You should also look at your opponent's moves and think about their
purpose. Are they trying to set up a travel?

Of course, this is also good advice for normal chess. But it's extra important in 5D, because winning travels can come
seemingly out of nowhere. You want to defend these things before they become a problem. This isn't to say that you should
never allow weaknesses. If you try to go the whole game without ever creating any weaknesses in your position, you will
probably play too passively and fall behind on piece development. It's okay to make a weakness, as long as you are aware
of it and don't allow your opponent to exploit it.
Piece Values:

Normal chess wisdom assigns a "point value" to each piece: pawn = 1, knight = bishop = 3, rook = 5, queen = 9. The piece
values are different in 5D, although no exact values have been agreed upon.

     Bishops are very strong in open positions where they can make long travels onto weaknesses near the enemy king.
     Knights can be strong defenders, particularly when placed on f3 or f6. They can also be strong offensively, if they

         can find a way to get close to the opposing king.
     Rooks are difficult to use in the early game, but in the late game they can become extremely valuable for their ability

         to travel an unlimited distance into the past.
     Queens are by far the strongest piece, even more so than in normal chess. Almost no material gain is worth the

         sacrifice of a queen, unless that sacrifice directly wins the game.
Important Tactics

Aside from normal 2D chess tactics such as a fork or a pin, there are some special 5D tactics that are important to
understand if you are competing against strong players.
The first of these is Phasing. This is an attacking tactic that is hard to see for beginners, but is very important not to ignore.
The idea of phasing is that a bishop can "pass through" a pawn when it travels through time, if the pawn was moved on the
correct turn.

                                   Black's bishop appears to "pass-through" the f-pawn as it moves back in time. This travel will land on f2

This tactic allows you to attack with a bishop even if there appear to be pawns blocking it. It is useful for attacking the f-
pawn, or for checking the opponent's king if they left the e-file open.
To understand phasing, try playing it on a board against yourself to see how the timing works out. Then, when you see your
opponent pushing a pawn in front of a bishop, you'll know when to watch out for a travel.
Another important tactic is the Anti-Castle Travel. If you castle your king into a position where there is an undefended
pawn, then you just gave yourself a weakness (Weakness #2)! This can be attacked like so:

                                             White castles into an undefended position, so black travels to attack the king in in parallel

This tactic is so strong that it is usually considered bad to castle in 5D chess. But castling can still be a good move! Just make
sure that you castle into a place that is already defended.
Travels and Counter-Travels

Sometimes you can go a whole game without any time travel, just sparring back and forth on one timeline. But sometimes...
travels happen. Usually, if the players are good, this will end up with somebody getting softmated. But, as every beginner
finds out, the game does not end there! If you are softmated on one timeline, you can still make a time travel to the past
from another timeline:

                            Softmate

                                                                White must
                                                                travel from
           here White ignored their weakness on f2 and allowed a softmate to happen. Now they must travel back to before the softmate

We call this type of travel a counter-travel. Now, if you are the one who has been softmated, you have two options:
    1. Win the game before the Present catches up to the board where you are softmated
    2. Maneuver a piece so that it can take the piece that is softmating you (we call this a recapture)
Obviously option 1 is better, but sometimes option 2 is the best you can do. Here is an example of a recapture, continuing
from the image above:

                             White travels back to before the softmate, and is now able to recapture with the knight from the bottom timeline

After the recapture happens, both players have spent one travel, so neither player has timeline advantage. Of course, the
position might still be winning for one player. Usually it ends up being better for the player who is attacking, because the
defender has to waste tempo to make the recapture.
If you see that your opponent is about to make a strong travel, and there's nothing you can do to stop it, you should try to
set up a counter-travel. For example, if you move a rook or queen on a turn before they travel, you will be able to send your
rook/queen very far into the past, and thus have a lot of turns to checkmate or set up a recapture. We call this tactic a
Jurassic Rook (or queen).
Ideally, you should consider the possibility of a counter-travel before you even make the travel in the first place.
Playing on Many Timelines

In the aftermath of the examples above, we are left with 3+ timelines and hundreds of possible combinations of moves.
How can we possibly analyze a position like this? If you know what to look for, these positions should be no more difficult
than a single timeline game.
Again, the key is to focus on weaknesses. Look at the position, including the history of the game, and ask yourself: Are there
any paths to my king? Do I have any undefended pawns near the king? Likewise, what weaknesses does my opponent
have?
The only difference is that now there are more dimensions to work with, and a lot more history to consider. Take look at
this game:

                                  White got softmated on the top timeline, but managed to make a jurassic queen. How can white win here?
Let's look for open paths to the black king (Weakness #1). Suppose we are aiming for the king on the top timeline (circled in
red).

If we look at all the squares adjacent to it (including ones on other timelines), we can see that there is a gap: d7 on the
middle timeline (marked in green) is unblocked. If we follow that triagonal down to the bottom timeline, we see that c6 is
also unblocked. Thus, there is an open path to the king!
If we could make another timeline with a queen on b5, then this should cause a check to the king on the top timeline. Can
we get a queen to b5? Yes! We can travel the queen from the bottom timeline two turns back and two squares up, landing
on b5:

                                                       The queen passes through the gaps on c6 and d7 to softmate the black king

At first it may seem impossible to find this kind of tactic, because there are so many possibilities. But with a bit of practice,
you will know exactly which squares you need to check, and which openings you need to defend.
Also, wait a minute... why isn't the game over? Oh no, black has even more counter-travels! But that's a story for another
time.
You are now good at 5D Chess with Multiverse Time Travel

Now you know enough to become a strong player! If you have read and understood this guide, then you are probably
already above the average player. The best way to develop your skills from here is to play lots of games against other
people at or above your level.
Note: it is less helpful to practice against players (or bots) who don't understand the fundamentals of strategy, because you
will be able to destroy them with this level of knowledge. You should teach them how to get good, then practice against
them. But don't be rude: it's not fun to learn by getting f7-sacrificed 10 times in a row.

Also, here is a list of the important concepts from the guide for review:
     Don't travel without good reason, because it gives away timeline advantage.
     Look for weaknesses in the opponent's position.
     There is a weakness in the starting position.
     Every move should have a purpose.
     If you get softmated, try to set up a counter-travel for a win or a recapture.

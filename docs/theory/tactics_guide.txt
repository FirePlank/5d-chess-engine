5D Chess Tactics
By:Mage
This is a simple WIP guide to common terminology relating to tactics and openings, shown with some images. The tactics for this guide are focused on the Standard - Turn Zero variant, but many of the tactics carry over to every other variant in the game.
Disclaimer: These tactics and the situations they are shown in are not necessarily good or optimal, but do show the tactic. Some of the situations are referenced from real situations and are linked.
Defended Travel:
A very basic travel where a piece like the queen can be backed up by itself to attack an unprotected square next to the king for immediate softmate.
Knight Paradox:
Related to the defended travel, a knight reroute can allow the knight to defend itself, which is useful for mating the king from 2 squares away.
Self-recaptures:
It's important to remember that pieces have the ability to recapture pieces that travel for a seemingly easy mate. For example, when only one timeline exists queens, bishops, and pawns can recapture travels that are 1 turn back, and knights can recapture 2 turns back.
Note 1: As an advanced counter to this, if your travel would leave the piece pinned, they will be unable to self-recapture.
Note 2: Oftentimes, when a self-recapture on a pawn would prevent a softmate you may want to look for an Auto-Tchaikovsky.

Stall checking: 
A common tactic where a player gives checks with the goal of gaining time to set up another tactic, time travel, time check, or delay until a future checkmate. This is usually at the cost of pieces without regard to their value. 
Example of stall checks
In the image above, after Nxe6 is played the diagonal to the king is forced open. In order for Qa4 to be a past checkmate, more time must pass without black protecting the square. So Bg6 is played as a stall check, then Be8 is played for another stall check before finishing with Qa4#.
Closing The Door:
This is a simple but useful tactic for calculating mates in 5D Chess. Whenever a piece is positioned for a travel or a king diagonal/triagonal is open the opponent can be forced to prepare an attack on the piece before it checks their king. However, by blocking the attack with a piece in between you can “Close the door” and stop their attack while allowing your travel to “pass-through” your piece.
Common Use of Closing the Door
Phasing:
This tactic can be tricky to time, but is very useful. By pushing pieces in front of bishops and queens, you can open up a diagonal through them in time. This looks like the piece “phases” through the ones in front of it as it slips through them.

Tchaikovsky (Anti-Castle Travel):
A common tactic that arises whenever the opponent moves their king on the main timeline towards a square that was not previously defended by the king and which has more attackers than defenders or an equal amount. By traveling to the turn before a king moves next to that square, you can check the king across timelines without being able to be taken by the king. This is most commonly done after a castle or after the king was forced to move.

Reverse-Tchaikovsky: 
An advanced and less common tactical travel which removes the king as a defender of a square in order to perform a Tchaikovsky on a king that didn’t move. This makes it the opposite of a Tchaikovsky.

Auto-Tchaikovsky: 
Another advanced and less common extension of the Tchaikovsky is Auto-Tchaikovsky where you sacrifice in order to drag the king towards a piece that has the ability to check the king in the past when the previous board is traveled to. This means that any travel to that past board will automatically cause a past check. This tactic is most common when a queen or bishop is 2 squares away from a king.
Example of Auto-Tchaikovsky
Knight-Loop: 
A simple knight travel to check a king parallel after dragging it out. It is essentially a Tchaikovsky with a knight. It is often called the knight loop because while it can be easy to capture and prevent the softmate it is also easy to continue repeating the same tactic like a loop because of the king’s positioning and the ability of knights to easily jump down 2 timelines unblocked.

Buffer-Timelines: 
An uncommon tactic that is used to separate timelines or change the distance so that the pieces will be unable to interfere across timelines or so that they will be able to mate. It can also be used in conjunction with exiling to make safer and further king timelines or it can be used against exiling to prevent your opponent from jumping to a nearby timeline. 
Note: One example of this tactic can be found in Advanced Branching II

Danrelle (Danrelle Counter Sac): 
A Danrelle is a tactic that becomes available when multiple timelines exist in which a piece from one timeline defends another across timelines—primarily used to refer to variations of its use in the opening as a counter to the f-sac.

Anti-Danrelle: 
A way to counter the Danrelle counter sac in the opening is by using a slower mating sequence which stops any setup for the Danrelle to work. This sequence begins with Nf3/Nf6 after an f-sac rather than Nh3/Nh6 and is one turn slower.

Pseudo’s Defense: 
A defense for black that counters a T1 f7-sac by traveling to T0 to land a piece in position to capture the piece on f2. The resulting position causes black to be behind in development on one timeline but up a queen overall and equal on timeline advantage. It is also a useful pattern to remember for recaptures of other similar mating travels.
Pseudo’s Defense used to stop a deadly f-sac
Parallel Queen Defense: 
A defense that can be used against the f-sac in which you prevent mate by using a queen that is in parallel to protect the squares the opponent can mate from. It can be useful to protect against all sorts of mating sequences.
Note: This tactic does not prevent a T2 inactive softmate. So it is often not recommended for higher-level play.
Jurassic(Jurassic Queen/Rook): 
The use of a rook or queen to time travel far into the past. This tactic is usually used in emergency situations to escape from dangerous positions and to threaten counter travels.

Multi-timeline diagonals/triagonals/quadragonals: 
When multiple timelines are created the openings in an opponent's defense start to show. Openings through the diagonals of the kings that get long can be easy to exploit with enough time travel advantage but can be hard to spot without keeping an eye on them.

Time-Trapping: 
A multi-timeline tactic to look out for after many travels have been made. By giving a check on one board you can “time trap” all the pieces on it and then threaten them from another board. Most commonly done to prevent a king or queen from moving on one timeline to threaten capture.
Example of time trapping. 
The top board is time trapped by using a bishop check so they have no way to save their queen.
5D Smothered Mate: 
The familiar smothered mate from 2D chess except that the king is also smothered in all the dimensions and has nowhere to escape to. This is best done with a knight traveling back two turns, otherwise, it's likely that the king will be able to jump to the future without using timelines to escape.
Exiling (Exiled Kings): 
The term exiling often refers to any king movement which creates a new timeline, but most often refers to traveling kings until all of them are on inactive timelines. It is both a strategy and a tactic and works well in combination with Inactive Softmate. After the kings have been exiled their past squares can be covered so that the opponent must time travel in order to activate the boards with kings on them if they want to win. 
As a strategy, it can be difficult to deal with for new players, however, it does give a lot of timeline advantage which can be used to counter it. 
As a tactic, it can be useful to exile kings for the purpose of avoiding otherwise unavoidable checks or mates.

Inactive Softmate: 
Identical to regular softmate except it takes place on an inactive timeline. Since the timeline is inactive the opponent is not required to deal with the board and can continue playing on the active timelines. However, it prevents the opponent from making any time travels that are not before the inactive softmate. This can be useful to prevent the opponent from traveling. It is often used in conjunction with exiling so that the opponent will be forced to deal with the inactive softmate if they want to win because if you have no king and no open paths to past kings they will be unable to mate without traveling before the inactive softmate.

Common Mating Patterns
f-sac(f7/f2 sacrifice): 
An infamous mate/mating pattern that specifically refers to landing on f7/f2 on T1 or T0, but the pattern it uses can be used in a wide range of positions. It is the fundamental reason behind the limited opening variety of the t0/standard variants.
Without interference from other timelines a travel onto f7 on T1 or f2 on T0 is guaranteed mate in 4 after the king takes on f7. 
The sequence begins with Nh3 to allow the queen to pass through f3 while also allowing for a check with the knight. 
Shown below is what happens if black responds with Nf6 to block the f-file for the king.
If instead black responds with e6 to block the diagonal to the king there are two lines to remember:
1. Mate on h5. The opponents attempt to stop Qf3# in the coming turn with Qf6 but leaves h5 undefended and Qh5# occurs a turn earlier than Qf3# would.
2. Mate on f3. The opponents attempt to stop Qh5# after e3 by playing g6 or Nf6. After this Qf3 threatens an unstoppable mate if the opponent is able to block with Qf6 you can play Ng5# which provides two checks at once for hardmate
Deviations from these lines like Ke8 or d5 all fail to the same lines.

1. xf7 / Kxf7 2. Ne5+ / … 3. Qh5#: 
A typical mating pattern where you simply check once with a knight after dragging the king out and then Mate with the queen now that you’ve delayed one turn.
1. xf7 / Kxf7 2. Bxf6+ / … 3. Qh5#: 
Similar to the 1. xf7 / Kxf7 2. Ne5+ / … 3. Qh5#  pattern where you check and then play Qh5#. However, it can be trickier to spot because it removes the knight off of f6 with a check to allow Qh5#.
1. xg7+ / Kxg7 2. Ng5#: 
A pattern that is useful against castles especially when you might miss your chance at a Tchaikovsky.

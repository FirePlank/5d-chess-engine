Common 5d Chess Openings In Turn 0:
By: PseudoAbstractMeta & Los Bro SPQR
This document explains three playable openings in 5d chess which stop the f7/f2 sacrifices on turn 0. The different openings in 5d chess haven’t been thoroughly explored, but we hope that these setups can help get people into interesting positions. 
There are also other ways to play the opening, such as by allowing the f7 sacrifice, and there exist other move orders to stop such f7 attacks. The methods described here, however, are likely the safest ways to play the opening as they guarantee positions that can easily be studied and analyzed.
If you wish to learn more about why the openings are played as they are, please message PseudoAbstractMeta, Los Bro SPQR, or other people in the 5d chess discord at https://www.5dchesswithmultiversetimetravel.com/discord.
Move 1:
It is currently believed that the best first move for both sides is to develop the kingside knights to f3 and f6 respectively.
This is thought to be the best opener because this move stops a lot of threats that exist from the starting point of the game. The main tactic that the knights stop is a possible bishop sacrifice which is done by time traveling the bishop on f1/f8 from turn 7 to f7 or f2 on time 1 or 0, which would create a timeline where the king is too exposed and allows for the potential of forced mating sequences or the ability to remove the kings from play, famously known as the exiled kings strategy. The knights block the entry way that the bishop would travel along on turn 2, so either side no longer needs to worry about such threats. The other big threat that lurks in the position is that a queen on h5 or h4 can create similar time sacrifice threats on f7/f2 which the knight covers. For more in depth information please check this guide on f7 sacrifices which was created for the original standard variant- https://steamcommunity.com/sharedfiles/filedetails/?id=2218187793. 
To simplify this guide, I will mainly be referring to the f7/f2 sacrifice on turn 1/turn 0 as an f7 attack to make the text easier to read.
Move 2:
White and Black have the choice to either push their d pawn or their c pawn in order to defend against the f7 attack. If White’s queen were to end up on b3, or Black’s queen were to end up on b6, then they have the potential to go for an f7 attack.
Move 2, Pushing d4:
The goal of pushing d4 with White and d5 with Black is to immediately block the diagonal to f7/f2. Very often the move c3 or c6 is played afterwards  in order to close off the now open diagonal that leads to the king. After White plays d4, Black will most likely respond with d5 and play symmetrically.
This is the main starting position which occurs after 1. Nf3 / Nf6 2. d4 / d5 3. c3 / c6. It is currently the most played opening in Turn 0 because of how solid the position is for both sides. We have begun calling this type of setup the London, because it shares a similar structure to the London System in traditional chess.
Although it is also possible to block the diagonal towards the king with a move such as b4, Bd2 or Nc3, they generally run into weaknesses. if White were to place their knight onto c3 and then move the knight, then their king would be open to a unicorn mate by the queen on a5. 
Similar traps can occur if the bishop were to move after it is placed on d2. However, these moves have not been looked into too deeply, so it may be viable to play them with the correct setup. 
Move 2, Pushing c4:
Pushing the c pawn intends to stop a queen from making an f7 attack by getting the queen to b3/ b6 before the opponent does and trading queens if the other side attempts to force the issue. If, for example, black where to respond to 2. c4 with / c6, then white could immediately respond with Qb3 and exchange queens if needed.
(Example variation where queens get traded off early.)
One of the benefits of pushing the c pawn to c4/c5 as opposed to the d pawn, is that you can safely place a knight on c3 or c6 without putting your king in danger. However, pushing the c pawn also creates some complications. for one, if the c pawn is placed in the center on c4/c5, then the side with that pawn has blocked themselves from being able to perform an f7 attack. Thus the other side doesn’t need to worry about an f7 sacrifice and has more choice of how they choose to develop.
The simplest way to play the position as black if white opens with c4 is to play symmetrically with c5, because it stops the c pawn from advancing, which can be a pretty big threat if the queen is on b3.
This is one of the starting positions of c4 / c5 which is dubbed the English. These positions have some potential for opening traps due to the activity of the knights and queens. 
Additional choices for Black:
Black additionally has the choice to play asymmetrically against whites' setup, which allows black to create an imbalance in the position. In traditional chess, it is considered that if black tries to play symmetrically to white, white should be a little bit better as they get to attack first. Black has the ability to begin playing asymmetrically from turn 2 to imbalance the game by playing the opposite pawn move.
Black move 2. c5 against d4:
By meeting d4 with c5, black’s tries to start an immediate attack by creating tension in the position. Note that 3. dxc5 doesn’t win a pawn because of 3. ..., Qa5+ and 4. …, Qxc5. This is a very aggressive opening for black, but black can sometimes end up being down in development. White also has threats of their own, as currently black hasn’t defended against f7. White has several ways to continue from here but generally the next move for both sides are 3. c3 (to threaten an f7 attack and close the opening diagonal to the king) / Qb6 (to stop the white’s queen from getting to b3 and performing the sacrifice). 
This is currently the main opening moves of what is called the Sarperen’s Counterattack, as he is the first to play it in rated matches. Both sides have stopped each other’s immediate threats, but there are some complications in the coming moves, as blacks active queen and knight can start attacking whites position. 
If white does not wish to deal with such complications, then they can trade queens by playing Qb3. if black doesn’t want to allow an f7 attack, then black must exchange queens here.
